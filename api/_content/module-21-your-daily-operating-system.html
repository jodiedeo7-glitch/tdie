The build is done. What's left is the part that decides whether this compounds or dies — knowing what to do on an ordinary Tuesday when nothing is urgent.

Without a cadence you publish in bursts, go quiet for six weeks, come back to a site that lost momentum, and conclude it didn't work. It worked. It just needed input at a rate you can actually sustain.

Everything below assumes 2–4 focused hours a day, because that's what real people with businesses and lives actually have.

Daily — 2 hours

Morning block, 90 minutes — one thing that compounds. Writing an article, running SEO passes, building a resource. One task, no switching. This is the block that builds the asset.

Afternoon block, 30 minutes — circulation. Schedule pins, post to your community, answer comments. Timeboxed and hard-stopped. This work expands to fill whatever you give it and it does not compound.

Not daily: checking analytics. Once a month.

Weekly

One supporting article published. One. This pace produces 50 articles a year, which is a substantial site.

One repurposing pack run on that article. Fills the pin queue for the coming weeks.

Thirty-minute review, same time each week: What published? What's in the queue? What's blocked?

Fortnightly

One pillar article. Pillars take longer than a week to do properly and rushing them defeats the purpose. One pillar per fortnight is 26 a year — more than the 8–12 you need.

Monthly

Check Search Console. What are you appearing for that you didn't target? Those become next month's articles.

Test your own email signup. Ninety seconds. The one check that catches a silent break.

Check your bridge pages. Do the outbound links still go somewhere real?

Quarterly

Verify affiliate products still exist at the price you published.

Audit for broken links.

Review pillar performance. Which are pulling? Which have empty paid slots? That's your product roadmap.

Run the cut list.

The cut list

The discipline most systems lack. Once a quarter, ask what to stop.

Cut a pillar with fewer than 3 supporting articles a year after launch. That's not a backlog, that's a topic you don't want to write about.

Cut a platform you've posted to for six months with no measurable traffic.

Cut a product that hasn't sold in two quarters and isn't strategically necessary.

Cutting is what makes the remaining work sustainable at 2–4 hours a day. A system that only accumulates eventually collapses under itself.

What stays manual, permanently

Some things should never be automated:

  • Final approval on anything published. Read it before it goes out.
  • Community replies. People can tell.
  • Anything involving a price or a claim. Check it against your Canon File yourself.
If you get stuck
I fell off for a month

Nothing was lost. Your articles kept ranking and your pins kept circulating — that's the entire reason for building assets rather than a feed. Restart at one article a week. Don't try to catch up; catching up is how people quit twice.

I can't do 2 hours a day

Do one hour, three days a week, and accept the slower pace. Consistency at a low rate beats intensity in bursts, because the assets compound either way and only one of the two survives contact with a busy month.

Before you move on

A written cadence you'll actually follow, in your calendar.
