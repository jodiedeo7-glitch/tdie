What you're building on

Three things, and only one costs money:

GitHub — where your site's files live. Free. Think of it as a folder in the cloud that keeps every version of everything, forever.

Vercel — turns those files into a live website. Free at your traffic level, and "your traffic level" means well past your first year.

A domain — the address. $10–15/year from any registrar.

Total cost: about a dollar a month.

Why not WordPress

WordPress runs about 40% of the internet, so this needs a real answer.

WordPress is a database that assembles your page every time someone visits. That means hosting fees, plugins, plugin updates, plugin conflicts, security patches, and a site that gets slower as it grows. You'll spend real hours maintaining a system whose only job is to display text you already wrote.

The setup here builds your pages once, in advance, and serves them as finished files. Nothing to assemble on demand, nothing to hack, nothing to update. It stays fast at 500 articles.

Why not Beacons, Kajabi, Squarespace, or a Skool "about" page

They're fine, and you may already use one. The problem is ownership and control.

You can't edit what you can't reach. On a builder platform you get the fields they give you — meta descriptions, schema markup, URL structure, redirects, and internal link patterns are frequently locked or absent. Those are the exact things that make a blog rank.

Worse, link-in-bio platforms carry the aggregate reputation of everyone else using them. If enough spammy pages share your platform's domain, traffic sources start treating links from it as suspect — and your entirely legitimate page inherits a problem you didn't cause. Pinterest is particularly quick about this. On your own domain, your reputation is yours alone.

Keep the builder for checkout. Move the content to a domain you own.

Why Astro

Astro is the framework Claude will use to build your pages. You don't need to understand it — but here's what matters: Astro ships almost no JavaScript to the visitor. Pages load nearly instantly, and page speed is a ranking factor Google actually publishes.

It also has a layout system. One file controls the design of every article. Change that file, change 40 pages. Module 6 is entirely about this, because it's the difference between owning a website and owning a website system.

01

Do this

1. GitHub account. github.com, sign up, verify your email. Free tier.

2. Create a repository. Green "New" button. Name it after your brand, lowercase, no spaces — yourbrand-site. Set it to Private. Check "Add a README file." Create.

3. Vercel account. vercel.com — sign up with GitHub, not email. This is what lets them talk to each other.

4. Domain. Any registrar. Buy the .com if you can. Don't buy add-ons; you don't need their email hosting, their site builder, or their SEO package.

Stop there. Don't configure anything, don't import anything, don't connect the domain. Module 7 does that in the right order.

If you get stuck
It's asking me to choose a plan

Free. Every time, on both. Vercel's Pro tier is for teams and commercial usage — not you, not yet.

Should the repo be public or private?

Private. Your published site is public either way; the repo holds drafts and configuration. Private costs nothing.

I already own a domain but it's used

Fine. You'll use a subdomain — learn.yourdomain.com or blog.yourdomain.com. Module 7 covers both cases. Don't move your existing site.
