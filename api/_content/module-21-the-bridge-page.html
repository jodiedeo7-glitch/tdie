This module is why the website matters at all, and it's the piece almost nobody building a Skool group has.

The problem

You want to recommend things — an affiliate product, a tool you use, a partner offer. The instinct is to put the affiliate link straight in the pin, the bio, the post.

Three things go wrong.

Platforms distrust raw affiliate links. Pinterest in particular. Enough outbound affiliate links from one account and your links get flagged, suppressed, or your account restricted. The damage is rarely announced — your reach just quietly drops.

You learn nothing. A raw link tells you a click happened. Not which pin, not which article, not what the person read first.

You own nothing. If the affiliate program closes or the product is retired, every link you ever posted is dead, everywhere, permanently, with no way to update them.

The fix

Everything outbound routes through a page on your site.

Instead of pin → affiliate link, it's pin → your page → affiliate link.

That page is a bridge page. It's yours. It lives at a URL you control, it has real content on it, and it sends people onward from there.

Why this changes what your website is for

The bridge page is the mechanic that turns a website from a brochure into infrastructure.

Platform trust. Traffic goes to your own domain, which you've been building a reputation for with real content. It doesn't look like link-dumping because it isn't.

Analytics. You see which pin drove the page view and how many went onward. You can tell a winning pin from a losing one.

Permanence. Product retired? Edit one page. Every pin, post, and email you ever published now points at the replacement. Nothing else has to change. This alone justifies the site.

Capture. A bridge page can offer your free resource on the way past. A raw affiliate link cannot. You get value from clicks that don't convert.

Do this — build one

Build a bridge page at src/pages/go/[slug].astro using PageLayout.

Purpose: someone arrives from a pin about [topic] and I'm
recommending [product/tool].

Contents:
- Headline naming what this is and who it's for
- 200-400 words of real content on why I recommend it and what
  it's actually good for — including what it's NOT good for
- Clear outbound button to [destination URL]
- Affiliate disclosure above the button, plainly worded
- One link to my related article on [topic]
- The free resource offer for that pillar

Rules:
- rel="sponsored" on any affiliate link
- No fake urgency, no fake scarcity
- Real recommendation, honestly framed. Include the downside.

Do this — the disclosure

Above the button, not in the footer, not in small text:

Affiliate link — I earn a commission if you buy through it, at no cost to you. I only recommend things I use.

This is legally required in most jurisdictions and it converts better than hiding it. A visible disclosure signals you're not trying to be sneaky, which is exactly the doubt a reader has at that moment.

Do this — three rules for what you link

Never quote a sale price. You don't control the seller's pricing. Publish list price only, or no price. A quoted sale price that's expired makes you look careless.

Verify quarterly that it still exists. Affiliate products get retired, repriced, and pulled without notice. Fifteen minutes every three months.

Ship the link, the disclosure, and the rel="sponsored" as a set. Never one without the others.

Do this — beyond affiliates

Bridge pages work for anything outbound, and the reasons are the same:

  • Your own product on an external checkout
  • Your Skool group
  • A partner's offer
  • Any link you might ever need to change

Rule of thumb: if you'd ever need to change where a link points, it should go through a page you own.

Roadblock — "Won't the extra step lose people?"

Some, yes. What you get back is more than the loss: platform safety, analytics, permanence, and captured emails from people who weren't going to buy anyway. On any timeline past a month, the bridge page wins clearly.

Roadblock — "My links already got flagged"

Stop the raw links first. Build bridge pages, repoint what you can, wait out the platform's re-crawl window — usually a week or two — before appealing. Appealing while the flagged pattern is still live gets you denied and burns the appeal.

Deliverable

One bridge page live, with disclosure and rel="sponsored", and every outbound link you control routed through your own domain.
